### Post 2: Tue Oct 6 · The Safeguards Rule, in 9 lines

> The FTC Safeguards Rule, for a 10-person tax office, in plain English.
>
> 1. Name one person in charge of security. The rule calls them the "Qualified Individual". In a small firm it's often the owner.
> 2. Know what client information you hold and where it lives: tax software, email, portal, laptops, paper.
> 3. Limit who can see it to the people who need it.
> 4. Encrypt it, on devices and when it's sent.
> 5. Use multi-factor authentication, meaning a second step when anyone logs in, on systems that hold client information.
> 6. Securely get rid of client information you no longer need.
> 7. Train your staff so they recognize the obvious traps.
> 8. Keep an eye on your service providers: software vendors, your IT company, anyone who touches the data.
> 9. Know what you'd do if something goes wrong. If unencrypted information about 500 or more people is taken, the FTC must be notified within 30 days of discovery.
>
> Then write it down, and review it as your firm changes. That written document is your WISP.
>
> Some small firms are excused from a few parts of this. That's Thursday's post.
>
> Which of the 9 would be hardest at your firm? My guess is #8, but I'd like to hear from people who've done it.
>
> Not legal advice. Sources in the first comment.

**First comment:** 16 CFR Part 314 (eCFR), FTC guide, FTC blog on the notification requirement (May 2024).
**Legal-ops check:** the 9-item simplification against 16 CFR 314.4(a)–(j).

### Post 3: Thu Oct 8 · Under 5,000 consumers

> "We're a small firm, so the Safeguards Rule doesn't really apply to us."
>
> That's partly right, and the part that's wrong matters.
>
> If your firm holds information on fewer than 5,000 consumers, the rule may excuse you from four things:
> • a written risk assessment
> • continuous monitoring, or yearly penetration testing
> • a written incident response plan
> • an annual report to a board
>
> Everything else still applies. Including:
> • having a written information security program (your WISP)
> • naming a Qualified Individual
> • multi-factor authentication
> • encryption
> • staff training
> • overseeing your service providers
> • notifying the FTC within 30 days if unencrypted information on 500+ people is exposed
>
> The 5,000 count is consumers you hold information on, not clients you serve this year. Past years' returns sitting in your software count too. If you're near the line, ask your attorney how to count.
>
> Good news for small firms: your WISP can be shorter. It just can't be missing.
>
> Not legal advice. The exact text is 16 CFR 314.6, linked in the first comment.

### Post 5: Wed Oct 14 · A plan vs. proof

> A WISP says what your firm does to protect client data.
>
> The harder question is: could you show that it's true?
>
> I think about each safeguard in three buckets:
>
> ✔ Verified: you could show someone. For example, you can open your email admin page and see MFA turned on for every user.
> ◐ Owner-stated: you believe it's in place, but you haven't looked. "I'm pretty sure the laptops are encrypted."
> ✗ Gap: it isn't in place yet.
>
> Most firms have a mix, and that's normal. The trouble starts when a plan says "Verified" about something that's really "Owner-stated".
>
> A 3-question self-test for this week:
> 1. Can you see, today, that every staff email account uses a second login step?
> 2. If a laptop were stolen tonight, do you know whether its drive is encrypted, or are you guessing?
> 3. Does your WISP name the person responsible for security, and have they read it?
>
> If you answered "guessing" to any of them, you've found your first fix.
>
> Tomorrow I'm releasing a free tool that walks through this in about 10 minutes.

### Reddit post A: week 2 (around Tue Oct 13, alongside LinkedIn post 4)

**Title:** Plain-English summary: W-12 line 11, the FTC Safeguards Rule, and what may be excused for firms under 5,000 consumers

> Renewal season is coming, so I put together a short summary of what the WISP question on the W-12 actually connects to. It's written for small practices. Corrections welcome. Several of you know this better than I do.
>
> *Disclosure: I work on security tools for small tax firms. There are no links to anything I sell in this post, and it isn't legal advice.*
>
> **1. What the W-12 asks.** Line 11 asks you to confirm you know paid preparers are required by law to create and maintain a written information security plan. You don't upload anything, but you are attesting that you know about the requirement.
>
> **2. Where the requirement comes from.** The FTC Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314, under GLBA) treats tax preparers as financial institutions. The main pieces:
> - a named Qualified Individual responsible for the program
> - knowing what customer info you hold and where
> - access controls, encryption, and MFA for anyone accessing systems with customer info
> - secure disposal
> - staff training
> - overseeing service providers
> - testing or monitoring, and reviewing the plan as things change
>
> **3. The under-5,000 exemption (16 CFR 314.6).** If you hold customer information on fewer than 5,000 consumers (past years' clients count too), four items may not apply: the written risk assessment, continuous monitoring or annual pen testing, the written incident response plan, and the annual report to a board. MFA, encryption, training and the written program itself still apply.
>
> **4. The FTC breach notice.** Since May 13, 2024, if unencrypted customer information on 500+ consumers is acquired without authorization, you notify the FTC no later than 30 days after discovery.
>
> **5. Free official resources.** IRS Pub 5708 (the WISP template, updated in 2024), IRS Pub 4557 (safeguarding taxpayer data), and the FTC's "Safeguards Rule: What Your Business Needs to Know".
>
> For those of you who have written a WISP: what part took the longest? I'm trying to understand where the real friction is.

### Reddit post B: week 5 (around Wed Nov 4)

**Title:** A vendor-neutral 10-question self-check before you tick the WISP box this renewal

> Following up on the summary I posted a few weeks ago. Several people asked for something more practical, so here are the questions I'd ask myself before renewing. No tool needed. *(Same disclosure as before: I work on security tools for small firms. Nothing to sell here, not legal advice.)*
>
> 1. Do we have a WISP document, and is it dated in the last 12 months?
> 2. Does it name our Qualified Individual, and does that person know?
> 3. Does it list the systems that actually hold client data today: tax software, email, portal, scanner, backups, any personal devices?
> 4. Is MFA on for every user, on email, tax software, the portal and remote access? Have I seen it, or am I assuming?
> 5. Are laptops and desktops encrypted? (BitLocker on Windows, FileVault on Mac.) Checked or assumed?
> 6. Do we send client files by portal or encrypted email, not plain attachments?
> 7. Did every staff member get some security training this year, even a short one, and is that written down?
> 8. For each vendor that touches client data, do we know what they're responsible for and how they'd tell us about an incident?
> 9. Do we have backups we've actually test-restored?
> 10. If something went wrong tomorrow, who would we call first, and do we know about the 30-day FTC notice for 500+ consumers?
>
> If you hold information on fewer than 5,000 consumers, some formal items may be excused (see 16 CFR 314.6). Most of this list still applies.
>
> What would you add? Especially anything specific to your tax software that people tend to miss.
